## The problem

Brazil is one of the world's farming giants, but much of its tropical soil is old, acidic and poor in nutrients. Farmers rely heavily on imported fertilisers and on lime to correct acidity, a costly cycle that also degrades soil over time. InPlanet's founders describe it as a negative feedback loop: more chemical inputs, diminishing yields and rising costs.

At the same time, the world needs durable ways to remove CO2. Enhanced rock weathering can do both jobs, but it works fastest where it is hot and wet. Most early ERW trials were in temperate Europe and North America, where weathering is slower. The tropics, with abundant basalt and intense rainfall, are where the method could scale most quickly. As the Negative Emissions Platform notes, Brazil's warm temperatures, high rainfall and acidic soils can all accelerate the reactions, and rock powder can reduce farmers' dependence on conventional fertilisers.

## The product

Felix Harteneck and Niklas Kluger met as students in Munich in 2013. Harteneck later ran a mobility and software business and went looking for credible carbon removal; Kluger worked on sustainable agriculture and family farming in Brazil. Their first talks about InPlanet happened while driving through Brazilian farmland, and InPlanet GmbH was founded in Germany in 2022.

InPlanet sources basalt, often a by-product of nearby mines, connects with farmers and arranges for the powder to be spread on their fields. Rainwater reacts with the basalt and captures CO2 as stable bicarbonate. InPlanet then measures, models and verifies the removal and sells credits. Its partners grow citrus, sugarcane, grains, coffee and pasture.

Its operations run on Bedrock OS, an AI-powered software layer that combines logistics, farm and laboratory data into auditable records. The company says it monitors everything from soil chemistry to local water systems, and shares anonymised data to support wider research.

## How it works

1. **Source.** Basalt fines are sourced from local quarries close to farms.
2. **Apply.** Farmers spread the finely crushed rock on their fields.
3. **Weather.** Warm temperatures and heavy tropical rain speed up the reaction with CO2.
4. **Store.** Carbon is converted into bicarbonate, which rivers carry to the ocean for long-term storage.
5. **Measure.** Soil, water and lab data are logged in Bedrock OS.
6. **Verify.** Credits are issued under Isometric's enhanced weathering protocol and listed on its public registry.

## Impact and numbers

InPlanet says it runs Brazil's largest ERW programme by farmland treated, with more than 12,000 hectares treated by the time of its Microsoft deal and access to more than 100,000 hectares through partners. By September 2026 it had applied more than 200,000 tonnes of silicate rock.

Its agreement with Microsoft covers more than 28,500 tonnes of CO2 removal between 2026 and 2028. All credits will be issued under Isometric's Enhanced Weathering in Agriculture Protocol. Over 24 months, InPlanet reported that treated fields showed higher soil fertility and reductions in fertiliser and limestone use. Its first verified credits, issued in December 2024, later received an "A" rating from the rating agency BeZero Carbon.

In September 2026, InPlanet and Idemitsu Americas Holdings, a subsidiary of Japan's Idemitsu Kosan, closed one of the first non-recourse project financings in the ERW sector. The debt funds delivery of the Microsoft contract and includes carbon delivery insurance from the specialist insurer CFC. Both firms say the structure is designed to be repeated, with Japan's and Brazil's emerging compliance markets in mind. Brazil's Law 15.042/2024 created a national emissions trading system that puts removals on the same legal footing as avoided emissions.

Other buyers and partners include AXA Switzerland and the Mercedes AMG Petronas F1 team. Microsoft's carbon removal director says the credits will contribute to the company's goal of being carbon negative by 2030. Commons, an Oakland-based app that helps people track the emissions tied to their spending, has supported InPlanet since 2025 and added its Brazilian credits to its portfolio after evaluating its measurement approach and data transparency.

## Honest caveats

**Measurement uncertainty.** Carbon from ERW is not seen directly; it is inferred from soil and water chemistry and models. Rigorous protocols help, but the science is still maturing.

**Company figures.** Hectare and yield claims come from InPlanet and its partners rather than independent long-term studies.

**Market risk.** Revenue depends on buyers such as Microsoft and on compliance markets that are still taking shape.

**Long-term effects.** InPlanet says it monitors soils and local water systems, but the effects of repeated applications over decades have not yet been studied at large scale.

**Logistics emissions.** Quarrying, grinding and trucking rock across a country the size of Brazil emit CO2, which must be deducted from removals.

## What's next

InPlanet and Idemitsu want to use their debt facility as a template for further financings across projects and buyers. Joining the Negative Emissions Platform gives InPlanet a voice in EU debates on certifying permanent removals. Expect expansion across Brazil's grain, coffee and sugarcane belts, and more peer-reviewed data from its fields.

## Why it matters for Europe and green buyers

For Europe, InPlanet is a German-founded company that shows how European capital and science can scale removal where it works best. As the EU integrates permanent removals into its climate framework, European buyers will want credits with strong verification, and tropical ERW offers more tonnes per hectare than many European fields.

For India, the parallels are strong: warm, wet regions, acidic soils, huge basalt deposits and farmers squeezed by fertiliser costs. InPlanet's model of local quarry rock, farmer partnerships and software-based verification is close to what Indian ERW projects are testing, and its financing deals show how such projects can raise capital.
